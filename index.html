Fuzzy Tiling Activation, Explained

A walk-through of Investigate the Properties of FTA Through Empirical Experimentation by Harsh Raj and Dilshad Shaik. One finding in one sentence: putting a tanh in front of a fuzzy tiling activation removes the need to hand-tune its tiling bound.

The problem: learning one thing makes a network forget another

A neural network learns by adjusting shared weights. When it learns from a stream of experience, as a reinforcement-learning agent does, an update that helps with today's situation can quietly damage what it learned about yesterday's. This is called interference.

Interference is worst when every input lights up most of the network's neurons (a dense representation). If each input used only a few neurons (a sparse one), updates for one input would mostly leave the others alone.

Fuzzy tiling activation (FTA), proposed by Pan, White and White, makes a layer sparse by design. The catch, which this paper tackles, is that it only works well if one number, the tiling bound, is set right.

How FTA works: try it

An ordinary activation such as ReLU turns each number into another number. FTA turns each number z into a short vector of k tiles. It splits the range from −u to u into k equal tiles and lights up the tile z falls in. Tiles just outside z light up partly, fading over a distance η (the "fuzzy" part), which lets the network keep learning through them.

Why the tiling bound matters

In a real network, the inputs to FTA are the outputs of the layer before it, and nobody knows their range in advance. Below, a layer produces 64 values with a spread you can change. Pick a tiling bound for plain FTA and see how many of its 20 tiles those values actually use; the right-hand card always uses tanh with u = 1. Unused tiles are wasted neurons; if all values crowd into one or two tiles, FTA stops separating inputs.

What the paper did

Reproduced the original resultA deep Q-network (DQN) learns to land a rocket in LunarLander. With FTA in its last hidden layer and u = 20, it did about as well as the original FTA paper reported.
Showed FTA is fragileSweeping u from 0.1 to 100: FTA did best near u = 1 and badly when u was too big or too small. The best u also differed between LunarLander and CartPole, so it has to be searched for each task.
Found a fixPutting tanh, Batch Norm or Range Norm in front of FTA. Only tanh worked at every bound tested in LunarLander. It also fixed the bad bounds in CartPole, and its results were the most consistent across random seeds.

Why this matters

Every hyperparameter an algorithm needs is a search someone has to run, in compute and time, for every new task. FTA's tiling bound was one of those. Because tanh squeezes every value into the range −1 to 1, a bound of u = 1 always covers the inputs, and the network can learn to spread its values across the tiles itself. The result is a sparse activation that works out of the box.

Limits: the paper tested two environments and one algorithm (DQN), with 10 runs per agent. Whether tanh-normalised FTA holds up in more tasks is open.
